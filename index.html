Ethereum and Distributed Settlement Infrastructure in Indian Institutional Finance

What this is: A six-module evidence base on where distributed-ledger settlement and tokenisation stand for Indian institutional finance, built for readers who have seen several blockchain pitches this year and were unconvinced by all of them. Every claim carries its source and date. Every figure carries a source tier. It is written to be checkable rather than persuasive.

The 30-second position

India already runs a blockchain in mandatory production in regulated finance. SEBI's Security and Covenant Monitoring System has recorded charges and covenants for corporate bonds on a distributed ledger since April 2022, operated by the depositories NSDL and CDSL, and no ISIN activates without it. The open domestic question is which process is worth the organisational cost, and on which rail.

On the rail, the evidence revised the working position. This research started from Ethereum as a neutral settlement layer beneath Indian finance, and that does not survive the production record. Every bank-run core-settlement venue with real volume chose a permissioned ledger and said so on the record, citing confidentiality. India's own near-term pilots settle in wholesale CBDC. Ethereum leads the global asset-tokenisation layer by value, which is the funds-and-stablecoins layer rather than the bank-settlement layer, and that distinction decides everything downstream. The defensible role for public Ethereum in India is narrow: GIFT City fund tokenisation, genuinely cross-border flows, and, once the VDA tax classification is settled, unit tracking inside an already-regulated fund wrapper.

The infrastructure claim survived intact. India's regulators mandate and pilot DLT infrastructure while remaining hostile to crypto as an asset. That is the line they actually draw, and the most important one to get right in any external conversation.

Findings by module

  • A — What shipped. Production tokenisation is confined to cash and short-term money: deposit tokens, money-market and Treasury funds, repo, stablecoins. Bonds, equities and trade finance are pilots or failures. The world's highest-volume system, Broadridge's repo platform at roughly $8tn in a single month, is permissioned rather than public. The graveyard is the part to read. TradeLens, we.trade, Marco Polo, Contour and the ASX CHESS replacement all failed, almost none of them for cryptographic reasons; they failed on consortium governance and competitor distrust, and the same failure modes are latent in India's IBBIC/IBDIC.
  • B — What's legal in India. No ban, and no law either, which for the institutional case are very different things. The binding constraint is unaddressed legal ground rather than prohibition: on-chain settlement finality and ledger-as-legal-register are not contemplated by Indian statute, and supervisory caution does the rest. Only the private-crypto route is genuinely restrictive, via a punitive tax, and that is not this case. The widest permitted perimeter is GIFT City rather than the domestic market. Check here before conceding that anything is "not allowed."
  • C — Where the value is. The largest rupee prizes and the nearest permitted pilots do not overlap, and that gap is the whole strategic problem. The big opportunities sit in the domestic RBI/SEBI perimeter where the law is silent, while the permitted pilots are offshore, tiny, or not yet live. Read it before sizing any opportunity, because for most of these processes the incumbent rail already clears the trade, which leaves fraud or capital mobility as the only honest marginal case.
  • D — Ethereum vs alternatives. The choice is three-way: public chains, multi-party permissioned (Canton), and single-operator permissioned EVM. The middle option is usually left out of the argument. Ethereum wins on uptime, counterparty reachability and India-available talent, and loses on confidentiality, cost predictability, permissioning granularity and legally recognised finality, which are precisely the criteria that decide bank settlement. This is the module to reach for when someone asks why not just use Ethereum.
  • E — The objections. Each objection is stated in its strongest form, with the honest response and the residual weakness that survives it. The regulatory objections are the strongest and mostly correct as stated, so conceding them is more persuasive than defending against them. The two an opponent is most likely to win are "every consortium blockchain failed" and "we already have UPI." Lead with the specific rupee problem and the named owner, never with the technology.
  • F — How adoption happens. Indian financial infrastructure adopts in a repeatable order: statute first, a regulator-owned utility second, a mandate or subsidy third, phased compulsion fourth, network effects last. A tokenised settlement layer sits pre-statute, roughly where dematerialisation sat in 1994. Demat scaled only under compulsion, and Account Aggregator, built without a mandate, is under 10% conversion after five years. A conference convenes and aligns; it does not procure.

Before anything leaves the room. The most-quoted number in this base, Ethereum's share of tokenised real-world assets, is contested across three modules at roughly 50%, 58–63% and 65%; the defensible phrasing is "the largest single share, roughly half," and the other two should be retired. No live Indian pilot has produced a measured rupee saving, which is the largest evidentiary hole here, and it is better conceded than papered over with a vendor figure. The Reconciliation tab covers both.

Reading guide and scope

This portal is a working research artifact, published openly so its sources can be checked. The summary tiers reflect the reconciliation pass; the full module reports are as originally researched. See the Reconciliation tab for every place two modules diverged and which figure to rely on.

How to read the modules. Each module opens with three tiers: a 30-second answer, a 5-minute summary, and the full report. The summary tiers carry the corrections from the reconciliation pass; the full reports are the modules as originally researched, with a note where a figure or framing was later revised. The Figure Ledger is the reusable spine, holding every quantitative claim with its source, date and tier, filterable and staleness-flagged. When two modules disagreed on a number, the Reconciliation tab records which to rely on. Nothing here should be lifted into an external document without checking that tab and the ledger first.

Source tiers, used on every figure: T1 primary (regulator, statute, filing, on-chain); T2 named data provider (RWA.xyz, DefiLlama, World Bank, BIS); T3 tier-1 financial press; T4 company blog or press release (promotional); T5 crypto media or aggregator (leads only, never a sole source).

Status labels, on every initiative: IN PRODUCTION (live, with a volume figure and date); LIMITED PILOT (live but restricted, named participants); ANNOUNCED ONLY (no evidence of live use); WOUND DOWN (discontinued, with date and reason).

A standing caveat. This subject moves monthly. Roughly a fifth of the figures in this base rest on the weakest two source tiers, and many carry an "as of" date more than six months old. The Figure Ledger flags both. Re-check anything flagged before it reaches an external document.
